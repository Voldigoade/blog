The Internet is replacing its locks. **Even before the thief is able to open them.**.

Today, when you connect to a HTTPS site, make a payment or exchange sensitive data, part of security is based on mathematical problems that are extremely difficult to solve with our traditional computers.

The problem is that a sufficiently powerful quantum computer would not play with the same rules.

## The danger is not the 2026 quantum computer.

The current quantum machines are far from being able to simply “break the Internet.” To break down large-scale systems such as RSA or some elliptical curve cryptographs, quantum-tolerant computers would be much more powerful than those available today.

But waiting for them to exist would be a very bad strategy.

Crypto data can be **They were intercepted today, preserved for years, and then decrypted later.** The technology becomes powerful enough. This is what is often called *by farvest now, decrypt later*.

In other words, a secret stolen in 2026 may still have value in 2036.

## Post-quantic cryptography has already existed.

In 2024, the NIST standardized several algorithms designed to resist both traditional and future quantum computers. **by KEM** The installation of keys and **by ML-DSA** for digital signatures. In 2026, the Organization now considers that migration should begin. 

And this transition begins to touch the real web.

For example, Chrome works on a roadmap to make HTTPS authentication resistant to quantum attacks. Google is also testing a new certificate architecture **The Merkle Tree Certificates**In particular with Cloudflare, to prevent post-quantic protection from making connections much harder. 

So the most fascinating thing is not that a quantum computer can one day threaten the Internet.

It is that **The defense against this hypothetical machine is already being deployed.**.

And if the transition succeeds correctly, the day when truly dangerous quantum computers will appear, the majority of users may not notice absolutely anything.
